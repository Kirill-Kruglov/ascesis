# Computability of Environment

## Status

Foundational analytical document.

This document does not introduce a substrate.

It studies the computational nature of environments themselves.

Its purpose is to characterise the space of computable environments before
evaluating individual substrate candidates.

---

# Motivation

The Justitia programme investigated one particular environment.

Substrate Discovery investigates the entire class of possible environments.

To perform such a search,
we first require a language capable of describing environments independently
of any implementation.

This document introduces that language.

---

# Historical Perspective

Machine learning traditionally treats the environment
as a source of observations.

Reinforcement learning models the environment
as a transition function.

Control theory models the environment
as a dynamical system.

Physics models the environment
as lawful evolution.

Program synthesis models the environment
as executable computation.

These viewpoints differ substantially,
yet they appear to describe related objects.

The present programme asks whether they can be unified.

---

# Observation

All candidate substrate classes considered so far
share one common property.

They generate observations through computation.

The computation may be

- deterministic,
- probabilistic,
- stochastic,
- adaptive,
- interactive,

but some computational process exists.

This observation motivates a more abstract definition.

---

# Working Definition 3.1

Environment

=

a computable generator of

- observations,
- interventions,
- consequences.

The definition intentionally excludes

- semantic interpretation,
- embodiment,
- implementation details.

Only interaction behaviour matters.

Status

Working definition.

Not established.

---

# Consequences of the Definition

This viewpoint immediately unifies

- cellular automata,
- physical simulators,
- executable programs,
- theorem provers,
- games,
- robotics simulators,
- stochastic dynamical systems,
- evolutionary simulations.

Their implementations differ.

Their interaction pattern is computationally generated.

---

# Computation versus Representation

An environment should not be confused
with its representation.

Examples

A chess engine

is not

the board position.

A physical simulator

is not

the rendered image.

A theorem prover

is not

the printed proof.

Representations are observations.

The environment is the process generating them.

---

# Interaction

Static datasets

contain observations.

Computable environments additionally support intervention.

Interaction therefore becomes
a defining computational property.

Without intervention,

counterfactual structure may never become observable.

---

# Minimal Computational Components

Any computable environment appears to require
at least four conceptual components.

State

Current computational configuration.

Transition

Rules generating future states.

Observation

Information exposed to the learner.

Intervention

Operations available to the learner.

Additional components may exist,
but these four appear fundamental.

Status

Observation.

Needs review.

---

# Beyond Determinism

Historically,

deterministic environments
were often considered the natural target
for reasoning systems.

This assumption is unnecessarily restrictive.

Many lawful systems
are inherently stochastic.

Examples include

- statistical mechanics,
- population genetics,
- queueing systems,
- Bayesian inference,
- Monte Carlo algorithms,
- Markov processes.

Randomness therefore does not imply
absence of computational structure.

---

# Working Distinction

Arbitrary randomness

has no stable computational generator
available to the learner.

Lawful stochasticity

is generated by stable computational laws
whose outcomes remain probabilistic.

Determinism

is a special case
where the generated distribution
contains a single outcome.

This distinction appears more useful
than the classical

deterministic versus stochastic

classification.

Status

Working hypothesis.

---

# Computable Environment Taxonomy

At the highest level,
candidate environments appear to fall into
several computational classes.

C1

Deterministic transition systems.

C2

Probabilistic transition systems.

C3

Lawfully stochastic systems.

C4

Interactive systems.

C5

Game-theoretic systems.

C6

Executable program worlds.

C7

Formal proof worlds.

C8

Physics-based worlds.

C9

Evolutionary worlds.

C10

Self-modifying worlds.

C11

Hybrid environments.

The classes overlap.

They are not intended as a partition.

Their purpose is descriptive.

---

# Richness

The computational richness of an environment
should not be confused
with implementation complexity.

Very small computational rules
may generate extremely rich behaviour.

Examples include

- cellular automata,
- recursive programs,
- dynamical systems.

Conversely,

large implementations
may produce little computational novelty.

Therefore implementation size
is not an informative measure.

---

# Computability Is Not Enough

A computable environment
need not be useful.

For Door-1,

additional properties appear necessary.

Examples include

- intervention,
- feedback,
- auditability,
- derivability,
- lawful structure.

These properties
are investigated separately.

---

# Relation to Goal Anchor

The Goal Anchor does not require

determinism,

symbolic reasoning,

or physical realism.

It requires

an environment
whose lawful computational structure
can in principle
be discovered through interaction.

This distinction motivates
the remainder of the programme.

---

# Immediate Open Problems

Can every computable environment
support internal-model construction?

Which computational classes
naturally expose causal structure?

Can lawful stochasticity
support derivation
as effectively
as deterministic dynamics?

Which computational classes
admit meaningful intervention?

Can computational richness
be increased
without sacrificing auditability?

These questions motivate
the following chapters.

---

# Summary

The central proposal of this chapter is modest.

Rather than viewing environments
as collections of observations,

we propose viewing them
as computational processes
generating interaction.

This proposal does not yet explain
which environments are suitable.

It merely establishes
the conceptual space
within which substrate discovery
will proceed.
